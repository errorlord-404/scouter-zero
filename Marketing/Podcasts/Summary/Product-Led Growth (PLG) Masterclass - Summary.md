## Overview

This episode is framed as a synthesized masterclass on Product-Led Growth (PLG), drawing on the extensive company deep-dive research both Akash and Jared have done across their newsletters. ## The PLG Iceberg Framework

### Origin Story

Jared created the PLG Iceberg while researching Canva for a deep dive. He wanted a metaphor that could explain why some companies seem successful on the surface but have enormous depth underneath. Watching *Titanic* (which he rewatches annually — his dream Lego set is the Titanic ship) sparked the analogy: every iceberg has a visible tip, but the most formidable ones are widest at the bottom and deepest underwater. The more dangerous and formidable an iceberg, the deeper and wider it goes — that hidden depth is what makes the best PLG companies so hard to compete with.

> "The companies that are doing PLG really really well have very clear tips but go really strong down the motion of how do you get into the product and discover the product and have all these pathways to monetization and flywheels."

The framework was first published in Jared's Canva deep dive, then applied in both their Adio and Miro deep dives, and continues to appear across their writing.

### The Eight Layers

1. **Core Problem Communication** — Can I tell from far away what you solve?
2. **Information for Decision** — Once I'm close, does everything convince me to try?
3. **How Easy Is It to Try (Friction)** — What does signup feel like?
4. **Value Discovery** — How easy is it to learn the product?
5. **Time to First Aha** — How quickly do I get value?
6. **Retention Loop (Repeatable Value)** — How often do I keep getting value?
7. **Monetization** — How well does the product capture value?
8. **Flywheels** — Do users bring in more users?

Key principle: these layers are **sequential**. Like Maslow's hierarchy of needs, you cannot effectively optimize a lower layer if the ones above it are broken. If nobody understands your core problem communication, incredible monetization work is irrelevant.

## Layer 1: Core Problem Communication

**Definition:** Everything above the fold on your homepage, landing page, or LinkedIn post. The hook. You want to communicate in the simplest, punchiest English: who you are for, what you do, and why they need you.

**Figma as the canonical example:**

When Figma launched, they positioned against Sketch (primarily) and Adobe Photoshop (for enterprise). Their above-the-fold values:
- **Collaborative** — Photoshop wasn't collaborative at all
- **Borderless** — Sketch was a Mac app, hard to share across platforms
- **Transparent**
- **Community-driven**
- **Open-sourced**

> "I feel like the figma example even to this day these resonate so much with people where people say hey I want to be on the web, I want to be collaborating, I want to have all these modern design practices."

**Positioning philosophy (from Dovetail):** There are two distinct positioning questions:
1. Why do you need *this type of solution* at all? (Market education — e.g., Apple convincing people they need the Vision Pro)
2. Why should you choose *us* over alternatives? (Differentiation — e.g., Figma's approach when people already know they need design software)

**Leo Tharesen's framework** (referenced): You're either 10x better, different, or cheaper than a competitor — always relative to constant customer needs. Jeff Bezos's version: "In 10 years time customers are not going to come to me and say I wish things were more expensive or I wish delivery was slower."

**Design as a subliminal signal:** Design quality communicates craftsmanship before a word is read. Because tools like Figma and Canva have made good design accessible, strong design is now table stakes. Weak design causes instant drop-off.

**Inverse relationship for big companies:** As companies grow, they often lose the urgency to communicate crisply. They ride brand recognition. Smaller companies must be extremely tight. Akash calls out that competing VPs at large companies often dilute messaging over time, pushing from first-order effects ("we save you time") to vaguer second/third/fourth-order effects.

## Layer 2: Information for Decision

**Definition:** Everything that builds confidence after the initial hook. The goal is to make people feel smart when they take this to their boss. The "nobody gets fired for buying IBM" principle — you need to de-risk the decision for champions within organizations.

**Adio (CRM) deep dive example:**

- **Positioning:** "Customer relationship magic" — subtly riffs on CRM, very intentional
- **Core message:** "Powerful, flexible, data-driven" — captures what makes Salesforce special (flexibility, power) while addressing Salesforce's weaknesses (complexity, cost, implementation time) and the weaknesses of challenger CRMs (easy but not scalable, no data model depth)
- **Show don't tell:** Their homepage leads with an interactive demo video showing the product, not a description of it. The demo shows enrichment happening, network graphs of contacts, email interactions
- **Logo tray strategy:** Features Coca-Cola (signals enterprise credibility) alongside modern next-generation software companies (signals the future of B2B). Dual signal: established and modern
- **Feature communication:** Features are presented with value tied to them, not just specs. "Creating your dream CRM" with "flexible templates that adapt to your use case" — jobs-to-be-done framing
- **Peppered testimonials:** Rather than a single testimonials section, they weave customer quotes throughout the scroll
- **Dedicated customer pages:** Shareable proof pages so champions can send a URL to their boss

**June (product analytics platform) — innovative social proof design:**

June's founder Enzo built their testimonials as screenshots of actual WhatsApp/chat conversations with customers. This does double duty: it's interactive and intriguing to click through, but it also signals that June actually has *relationships* with their customers — they're on WhatsApp with them.

> "It shows that June actually have relationships with these customers, they're talking to them, they have them on WhatsApp."

**Klarna as a non-homepage example:**

Klarna often encounters users at e-commerce checkouts. Their message: "Four payments at 1/4 the price of your cost, paid off in 6 weeks." In two lines at the point of decision, they communicate everything needed. This is PLG's essence: positioning so tight that people don't need to talk to sales or research on Reddit and G2.

## Layer 3: Friction (How Easy Is It to Try)

**Definition:** The experience between the homepage and actually being inside the product. Not about eliminating all friction — about finding the *right* friction.

**Good friction vs. bad friction:**

- **Bad (selfish) friction:** Asking for information that benefits marketing/sales but doesn't help the user get value faster
- **Good friction:** Information or steps that, while adding a brief hurdle, dramatically accelerate time to value once inside the product

**Adio example of good friction:**

Adio requires a *company email* at signup, and also prompts you to sync your email account. This feels like friction, but the payoff is immediate: once synced, the product immediately populates your entire contact graph — every person you've ever emailed, with relationship strength scores, last interaction dates, and full interaction histories. The friction was necessary and valuable.

**Elena Verna's contribution:** Raised industry awareness that you don't always need to remove friction. Friction at the right moments enables the value discovery and aha moment layers that follow.

**Roas (case study in the transcript):** Removed the CSV upload requirement during signup to reduce friction. Result: top-of-funnel went up, but conversion and retention went down. People who didn't bother uploading their data never got value, because the data upload was precisely what unlocked value for them.

**Notion's approach:**

Notion asks minimal questions (team or personal or school use), then drops you into a near-blank canvas with a guided walkthrough. It's deliberately bold to have such a sparse starting state. Their key friction feature: instant, easy import from Google Docs, Asana, and other tools. Integrations in the context of PLG are not just nice-to-have — they are one of the most important friction-reduction features a product can have.

> "Whenever people ask me what's a good level of friction, I'm like — are you Notion level of friction?"

**Credit card decisions:** Substack (and both hosts' newsletters) require a credit card for paid content. This was a deliberate friction choice. 5 years ago the trend was "no credit card required," but today — especially for expensive products or easily-pirated content — gating with a credit card can make sense. The advice: test friction on and off, don't just follow trends.

**Key question for the friction layer:** What is one thing you could ask or require *before* someone gets into the product that will make it 2-3x faster for them to get what they came for?

**Capturing intent:** The most underrated friction question is intent/persona capture. Knowing what persona is entering and what use case they have allows you to: (a) qualify leads for sales teams, (b) personalize all subsequent nudges and communication in the PLG motion.

## Layer 4: Value Discovery

**Definition:** The experience inside the product of *seeing* the promised land. The party analogy:
- **Friction** = getting into the party (queue, bouncer, check-in)
- **Value Discovery** = walking in and realizing the bar is over there, the music is great, you're in the right place
- **Time to First Aha** = someone comes to your table with your favorite drink exactly when you hoped

Value Discovery is where best practices matter *least* and product-specific uniqueness matters *most*.

**Canva's approach — multiple learning modes:**

Canva understands that people learn in different ways: doing, watching, or reading. Their design:
- Templates to flatten the learning curve immediately
- "Try Canva" — an interactive whiteboard file you can open and manipulate before creating anything. No template, just a canvas you explore by doing
- Contextual walkthroughs that appear *when you need them*, not upfront
- Pull, don't push — they follow your actions and show information contextually

> "Don't annoy users. Pull don't push. Listen and learn, don't talk. That's what great communication is." (From Command Bar, referenced by Jared)

**Adobe Photoshop vs. Canva — visual contrast:**

Photoshop drops you into a complex multi-panel professional interface with no guidance. Canva removes the complexity, shows templates immediately, simplifies the view, and only shows advanced tools when you go looking for them. The visual contrast is used frequently in Akash's writing as a reference point.

**Apollo.io case study (Akash's direct experience as VP of Product):**

The assumption was that an onboarding hub would solve value discovery problems. The onboarding hub was built and still lives in the product. But the actual breakthroughs came from:
- Putting the right cold email templates in front of people
- Surfacing the right inbox settings automatically
- Prompting people to warm up their inbox before sending sequences

These are all product-specific, contextual, default-driven improvements — not a generic onboarding checklist.

**Key insight:** The surfaces you own as a Growth PM (onboarding flows, walkthroughs) are often *not* the best tools for Value Discovery. Smart defaults, contextual revelation, and simplified views do more.

**Advancing users through tiers:**

Even within a single paid tier, there are vast differences in how much value users extract. A user who just signed up for an Enterprise plan is at the bottom of their expertise curve. The job is to continuously advance them toward power user status — through contextual education, surfacing advanced features at the right moment, Easter eggs, and integrations.

> "People want to feel like little Wizards in your product. I love being the Notion wizard at my company."

## Layer 5: Time to First Aha

**Definition:** The conversion event after value discovery — the moment where the expectation set by the homepage and signup is *actually delivered*. The handshake that proves the product does what it promised.

**Critical nuance:** The aha moment is not when someone pays you. It's when they get the value they came for. Monetization is a separate, later layer.

**Conversion event must drive retention:**

The aha moment only truly counts if it affects long-term retention. A good aha metric is one where hitting it causes a significant jump in day-30 or day-90 retention. Example: Notion's aha is probably not "created a document" but rather "shared multiple documents with team members" — because *that* is when the product becomes socially embedded and retention jumps.

**Miro deep dive (with input from Kate Syua and Elena Verna):**

Miro's signup flow:
- Single sign-on available (keeps friction low, allows personal emails unlike Adio)
- Progressively expanding form — collects: team type, role, work within the team, experience with Miro, intended use case, role level, company size, acquisition source
- The expanding form *design pattern* matters: if users see it as too many steps, they drop off. Miro has tested to find the right number of questions without causing significant drop-off
- **Key data point:** They tested an AI collaboration partner feature during onboarding. No improvement in metrics. The basics outperformed AI bells and whistles.
- **Aha metric:** Creating and sharing multiple boards with collaborators and bringing people in. Their activation metric focuses on the team collaboration event, not solo usage.
- **Persona-aware onboarding:** First person from a company gets a different experience than someone joining an existing team. Team members get walked into the right board with context about who's already there.

**Proxy metrics for aha:**

Not every product can measure the true aha moment directly. Backstage's aha is "got your first job offer/application result," but their proxy activation metric is: "submitted 3 job applications." They know that users who reach 3 applications have dramatically higher long-term retention, even if a job offer hasn't come yet. The team can actively drive people toward this proxy through:
- Job recommendations
- Re-engagement messaging for those who haven't applied yet
- Application assistance features

**The double-sided test for a good aha moment:**

1. Is it incredibly valuable to the customer? (User side)
2. Is it strategically valuable to the business? (Business side — correlates with retention, expansion, monetization)

**Product-market fit and the aha moment:**

James (from Command Bar) stated: if different pools of customers are finding completely different types of value in your product, you might be good at sales and marketing but you don't have product-market fit yet. True PMF means a consistent population of users is hitting the same aha moment and finding the same value.

**Sequential dependency:** You cannot effectively optimize time to first aha without first getting friction and value discovery right. Trying to skip ahead leads to leaky activation.

## Layer 6: Retention Loop (Repeatable Value)

**Definition:** How often users keep getting value after their first aha. The engine of the product. This is where single-player vs. multiplayer dynamics, and low-floor/high-ceiling product design, matter most.

**Low floor, high ceiling:**

The best PLG products are simple for beginners but have enormous depth for power users. Examples: Notion, Adio, Fortnite.

**Notion as B2B North Star for repeatable value:**

Notion drives repeatable value through multiple vectors:
- Removing the barrier to start new things (low friction to create new pages, databases)
- Extending into a full operating system for teams — planning, objectives, playbooks, Q&A, all inside Notion
- Unifying information and workflows — project tracking, wikis, documents, roadmaps, embedded Figma/Miro content in one place
- Aha moment stacking: first aha is collaborative documents, second aha is using it as a full operating system
- Advanced functionality: databases, views, filters, relations — and now charting (released the week before this episode)
- The Product Growth team (Akash's) uses Notion as their operating system across 4 team members

> "You have COOs and founders saying 'the number one thing that's going to drive success to my business is getting my employees to use Notion better.' You can build literally your entire business on Notion."

**Fortnite as B2C North Star for repeatable value:**

- Average user: ~2,000 hours/year in the product
- Top 15% of power users: ~4,000 hours/year
- The learning curve is effectively infinite — it takes 5,000-6,000 hours/year of practice to reach pro level
- Power users at 4,000 hours/year are not even close to pro — they still have a growth trajectory that keeps them engaged
- Key principle: the learning curve itself drives repeatable value

**Jared's "Triangle Framework" for deepening repeatable value:**

Rather than broad horizontal expansion (moving into entirely separate use cases) or pure depth (making the core product better), the triangle approach:
1. Identify the core aha moment/value
2. Expand in two adjacent directions from that core (not separate markets, but adjacent features/use cases)
3. Then connect those two adjacent expansions at the bottom, creating emergent value where the two interact (1+1=3)

Notion example: Instead of just building a standalone calendar app (which would be adjacent but separate), Notion built a calendar that connects to existing databases and roadmaps — so your calendar is natively tied to your projects. The connection creates emergent value beyond the sum of parts.

**Multiplayer as a retention amplifier:**

Products with multiplayer dynamics have a built-in retention advantage: the more team members using the product, the richer the data and experience for everyone. Adio: the more team members syncing emails, the fuller the picture of all company-level interactions with contacts. This creates a flywheel within the retention layer.

## Layer 7: Monetization

**Placement philosophy:** Monetization is the seventh layer intentionally. You need to get the first six layers right before you heavily experiment with monetization. A broken retention layer cannot be fixed with monetization experiments.

**The "Burgers and Shakes" framework (Jared's, origin unclear — may be inspired by another source):**

- **Burgers:** The core reason people come to your product. What you primarily price against (e.g., Dropbox = file storage, Adio = connected emails and seats)
- **Shakes:** Premium features that enhance the core value. Not the primary reason someone signs up, but what turns users into power users and creates compelling upgrade paths
- **The Happy Meal:** Burgers and Shakes working together to drive conversions and expansion

**Beehive as the monetization case study:**

Beehive (newsletter platform, competitor to Substack) uses a three-tier value architecture:
1. **Writing & Content** — just send a newsletter. Given away free, forever, with no subscriber limit
2. **Growth** — tools to grow your subscriber base (boosts, paid advertising network, etc.) — paywalled
3. **Monetization** — turning subscribers into revenue (ad network, paid subscriptions) — paywalled

**Beehive's pricing innovation:**
- $150/month flat fee for all of their paid tools
- Substack charges ~10% of paid subscription revenue — at Akash's scale (130,000 subscribers), Beehive would cost the equivalent of a couple people's salaries less per year than Substack
- All-in-one stack: replaces ConvertKit, Meta Ads, Mailchimp, and other tools — one platform for the entire writing business
- Clear competitive arbitrage: beehive is dramatically cheaper at scale because they charge a fixed fee, not a revenue percentage

**Beehive's business model:** Their fastest-growing revenue is their own ad network — Beehive writers advertise in other Beehive writers' newsletters, paying for subscribers, and Beehive takes a percentage.

**Reverse trial model (Beehive + Canva):**

Types of monetization models:
- **Freemium:** Free forever (Dropbox free tier, Beehive's free newsletter plan)
- **Free trial:** Full premium features for X days, then locked until upgrade
- **Reverse trial:** Free trial first (full premium), then drops to freemium. Used by Beehive (give all premium features, then at day 14 drop back to free tier)

Key: Beehive and Canva communicate clearly what you'll lose when downgrading — not as a scare tactic but as an honest show-don't-tell of the premium value you've already experienced. This dramatically improves retention because users have tasted the premium experience.

**Beehive's execution principles:**
- No in-product selling banners or aggressive upgrade emails — contextual upgrade prompts appear only when a user tries to perform an action that requires a paid feature
- Building in public: founder Tyler's personal newsletter documents the product journey, driving FOMO and organic acquisition
- Founder-led sales for large accounts: when Akash had 130,000 subscribers, he had to talk to a salesperson even before onboarding. At that scale, Beehive adds human touch appropriately
- Sam (Beehive AE) proactively reached out to Jared about migrating — relationship-driven even within a PLG model

**Never sell to your user — guide them:**

> "They never feel like they're being sold to in the product. They don't have these big banners that come up. In places where it's an upgrade path, it's always contextual — 'you want to perform an action here, like design better.'"

## Layer 8: Flywheels

**Definition:** Mechanisms through which users bring in more users, creating compounding growth. Sometimes called growth loops (popularized by Reforge) — but flywheels predate Reforge and were written about for decades. Jim Collins' *Good to Great* argued you need a single flywheel to build a company that lasts 100 years.

**Four types of PLG flywheels:**

1. **Invitation flywheel:** Users are naturally incentivized (or intrinsically motivated) to invite others. Examples: Zoom sending meeting links, Riverside sending recording invitations, WhatsApp invites
2. **Billboard flywheel (concept credited to James Shapiro):** Your product becomes a visible billboard when used. The branding is embedded in the customer's output. Examples: Stripe checkout pages (you recognize it's Stripe), Plaid bank connection screens, Command Bar appearing on websites, Intercom chat widgets, iPhone's "Sent from my iPhone" signature, Adio's "Sent with Adio" email footer
3. **User-generated content flywheel:** Users are encouraged to create and share content about the product. Examples: Duolingo streak shares, Tik Tok's UGC model, Notion users showcasing their workspaces
4. **Word-of-mouth flywheel:** Great product triggers organic word of mouth, which can then be operationalized and amplified

**Adio's four flywheels in practice:**

1. **Multiplayer invitation:** The product is dramatically more valuable with more team members syncing email — Adio makes this obvious and actively drives team invitations during onboarding
2. **Billboard (email footer):** "Sent with Adio" appears at the bottom of every email sent through Adio sequences on smaller plans. Every cold outreach email is free advertising. Enterprise plans can remove it
3. **UGC / community templates:** Adio has templates for CRM list structures and workflow "plays." The vision is to open these to the community (like Miro) so users can share "Here's how Product Growth runs their CRM" — builders want to show off what they built
4. **Word-of-mouth operationalization:** Gamification in B2B (rare), referral rewards (you get "computers" and other incentives for referrals), and a dedicated referral team page. Dog-fooding: Adio's own team uses Adio to sell Adio — the CTO (Chris, noted but last name not given; Adio CEO Nicholas, CTO Alex are both named) confirmed that dog-fooding has directly influenced product roadmap

**Adio dog-fooding flywheel (from Akash's deep dive):**

Akash's deep dive included conversations with Adio Head of Growth Alex Fail, CEO Nicholas, and CTO Alex. Both executives confirmed that how they configure Adio for their own sales process has directly influenced how they've built the product. Internal usage is an improvement flywheel.

**Figma dog-fooding example (from Chris, CTO at Figma):**

> "Everybody uses Figma. You leave feedback on the product every day. When you use your own product and you're your number one customer, you're going to care so much. You're going to see all these little issues to improve. Your level of empathy just shoots through the roof."

**Duolingo's flywheels (simpler, but powerful):**

1. **Data flywheel:** More learners → more behavioral data → better insights → better product. Example: Sam (on LinkedIn) analyzed learner data and saw intent signals for music and different learning experiences → Duolingo invested in Music → now one of their fastest-growing verticals
2. **Revenue reinvestment flywheel:** More paid subscribers → more investment in engineers, PMs, salespeople → better product → more users. Common in both B2C and B2B SaaS

**Key insight on flywheels:**

> "Simple beats fancy. You don't need to boil the ocean on mapping this out. You probably have some innate flywheel anyway. Define it, then look at how to push it faster."

Most companies already have flywheels; the work is identifying which ones fit your model and accelerating them.

## Who Should Do PLG, and When

### Jared's Controversial Take: PLG for B2B Is Overrated in the Early Days

Jared started forming this opinion from conversations with James (Command Bar). Command Bar started PLG, got the wrong customers, had poor conversion rates, and wasn't learning enough. When they switched to sales-led:
- Surface area of learning from customers shot up
- Retention improved dramatically
- Conversion rates improved dramatically
- Book-a-demo users had far more intent than free trial users

The argument: doing PLG too early can *destroy* enterprise value.

**Why sales-led growth is valuable early:**

Sales provides double-sided value simultaneously:
- You're selling *and* interviewing the customer at the same time
- You learn whether you're selling to the right person
- You help them implement and reach time to value
- You don't need any of the product layers of the PLG iceberg built yet — you deliver value manually
- The incentive to talk to customers stays high (versus PLG, where once someone is in the product the incentive to talk to them drops)

**The "do it by hand first" principle:**

If you can do something by hand (manually, without automation), do it by hand first. Then you know exactly how to productize it. Once you know how to sell and you have some degree of PMF, *then* PLG and growth become powerful accelerants.

**Apollo.io trajectory:**

Apollo moved from sales-led to product-led growth at approximately $1M ARR. That shift took them to $50M ARR.

### The Three Vectors for PLG vs. Sales Decision

1. **Lifetime Value (LTV) of the customer:**
   - Low LTV (e.g., $100/year) → sales economics don't work → PLG favored
   - High LTV → sales may make more sense, especially early

2. **Ease of selling to your customer:**
   - End user can sign up and use independently (like Canva) → PLG strongly favored (doesn't require CIO approval, can shadow-IT its way into companies)
   - Requires organizational approval, multiple stakeholders, procurement (like Rippling, Workday) → sales is necessary

3. **Ease of implementing the product:**
   - Simple, fast time to value → PLG works
   - Requires significant integration, technical setup, configuration → sales may be needed to avoid abandonment (Amplitude is cited as an example — a technical product that started with sales before adding a PLG layer)

### Two Archetype Trajectories

1. **PLG → Sales:** Start with SMB/consumer segment via PLG, layer sales as you move upmarket to Enterprise (most common)
2. **Sales → PLG:** Start Enterprise with sales, add PLG to address SMB where sales economics don't work (Amplitude's path)

**Interpret example:** Interpret has no PLG motion at all — fully sales-led. Custom model setup per customer, long implementation, technical product. PLG would not serve them well.

### "Product growth" ≠ "Product-led growth"

The model must fit the company's stage, price point, customer type, and implementation complexity. The sexy reputation of PLG (driven by OpenView and others over the past 15 years) does not mean it's right for every company at every stage.

## The Future of PLG

### Jared's Two Forecasts

1. **Hyper-personalization + Dynamic UIs:**
   Generative AI gives products more data about individual users. The next frontier is partially customizing the UI per user — reprioritizing components, hiding irrelevant sections, making certain features larger — based on who the user is and what they're doing. This was technically difficult before; it becomes accessible as AI matures.

2. **Interoperability / Outsourced PLG Layers:**
   Companies like Command Bar are building PLG-as-a-layer (user assistance, contextual help, product discovery) the way Stripe built payments-as-a-layer. Companies will be able to outsource parts of the PLG iceberg (especially friction reduction and value discovery) to specialist vendors. The vision James (Command Bar) described: Command Bar learns about a user across *multiple products* through a shared user model. When you arrive at a new product that uses Command Bar, it already knows your preferences and behavior from other products — radical personalization that travels with you across the web.

3. **Sales comeback (Akash's forecast):**
   Tools like Apollo, Clay, and Adio are making sales dramatically easier and more accessible. More founders and early team members will be able to do effective sales without large sales teams. Sales will become "sexy" again as a go-to-market motion.

4. **Product-Led Sales (PLG + Sales convergence) (Akash's forecast):**
   Tools like Pocus allow teams to set up signal-based plays. Example: "Akash came onto Beehive, he has 130,000 subscribers, sends 4 newsletters per day → this is a signal to reach out about the Ad Network." Product usage signals trigger precisely targeted sales outreach. This is the dominant motion Akash forecasts for the next 5-10 years.

> "Even the sales-led motion tends to fall down if your product isn't self-served. How many times has our customer success team not been able to get a hold of key customer users even after selling a $100,000 deal? It happens every single day in Enterprise."

## Hot Takes on Product Management

### 1. Most People Should Say No to AB Testing More Often

The nuance: AB testing is not bad. The problem is people use it as a **lazy prioritization substitute**.

Issues with over-relying on AB testing:
- Tests have real costs: engineering time, design time, analysis time, opportunity cost (things you're *not* building while you're testing this)
- People test things without a clear "if-then" hypothesis
- Teams plan quarterly "5 AB tests" without thinking about why those 5, not 6 or 3
- It becomes a crutch to avoid having strong opinions: "Just test it" bypasses the hard thinking

> "Just because it's an AB test doesn't lower the pressure for you to write an amazing PRD and do the right product discovery process. If anything, you should be applying a higher bar."

**Jared's test for whether to AB test:** Does it:
1. Solve a real user problem?
2. Give you an if-then signal to progress your roadmap?
3. Move your top-line metric?

If not one of these three, don't test it.

**Unshipping culture:** Regularly retro your test backlog. If tests ran and were never actioned or discussed, that's a prioritization failure.

### 2. People Index Too Much on Strategy vs. Execution

Strategy is fun — it feels productive, it's comfortable, it involves playing chess. But:
- Customers don't care about your strategy document. They care about what's in their hands.
- At early stages, 2-3 months of strategy planning is 2-3 months of not building and not learning
- Execution in the real world teaches you things no strategy document can

**Beehive as execution model:**
- Strategy is simple: be the all-encompassing newsletter platform and ship faster than anyone in the market
- That's the whole strategy. Now execute.
- Product roadmap prioritizes shipping speed, not customer feedback (customer feedback is last on the priority list)
- Result: writers choose Beehive because they can see the velocity of improvement

**Differential application by layer:**
- Value discovery: just get out and execute — prototypes in front of users, ship fast
- Monetization: taking a step back and being strategic often helps (too many companies end up with 15 pricing variants and a mess)

### 3. Growth Teams Can Be a Trap and a Band-Aid to Bad Product UI/UX

The trap is not growth teams themselves — it's when **core product and growth teams work in silos**.

- The highway analogy: core product builds the highway; growth builds the on-ramps to that highway
- The trap: core product says "it's the growth team's job to get people using this" and throws features over the wall
- Better model: both teams understand the on-ramps and the highway together. Core product PMs should care about how users find and enter their features

> "Growth teams need to get along with everybody else or else it goes both ways — core product not thinking enough about adoption features and UX and default states, but also growth teams not playing well enough to teach and enable and scale what they're learning." — Elena Verna (referenced post)

Akash estimates 80-90% of companies have room to improve on this collaboration.

## Advice for Product Managers

### Build Trust First — It's Your Number One Currency

Trust is the prerequisite for everything else a PM does. Without it:
- Engineers won't trust your decisions
- Managers won't give you autonomy or bigger projects
- You'll hit resistance on every proposal

**How to build trust early (practical steps):**

1. **Act like a complete beginner when you join:**
   - Ask every question, including the ones that seem obvious
   - Ramp up in *knowledge* before ramping up in *contribution*
   - The goal is to show you deeply understand the business, the customer, and how people operate — before you ship anything

2. **Earn your own onboarding:**
   - Don't rely on others to get you up to speed
   - Create your own plan, set your own goals, communicate them clearly
   - Report weekly to your manager on what you're learning ("This week I learned X from talking to Jeff")

3. **Go deep in an unnoticed area:**
   - Look for a part of the data or product that nobody is currently focused on (because they're busy)
   - Become the expert in that area
   - Surface a suggestion or recommendation from that expertise — signals initiative and depth

4. **Ask your manager what they're wrestling with:**
   - Not "how can I help?" (puts the burden on them to think of a task)
   - "What's on your mind right now? What are you working through?"
   - Then come back with a concrete suggestion or offer to take something off their plate
   - Do it better than expected — deep rather than surface

> "Trust is your number one currency as a PM. You can have the skill — the skill is table stakes — but if you don't have trust, you're just not going to be able to get anything done."

**Trust as a company health signal:** If your trust level is dropping at a company, it might be time to start looking elsewhere.

### Find and Pull Your Biggest Levers

The highest performers are not doing the most work — they know where to apply effort to create maximum impact.

**The comparative advantage model:**

A CEO hires a receptionist even if the CEO is technically better at scheduling and writing than the receptionist. Why? The CEO's comparative advantage is investor conversations (high leverage). The receptionist's comparative advantage is scheduling and admin. Misallocating the CEO's time to admin would be waste.

For any moment in the day as a PM:
- What is my best-suited strength *right now*?
- What would give me the most leverage?
- Sometimes it's going deep as an IC for a day
- Sometimes it's a 20-minute call that resets the strategic direction for an entire team's week

**Avoid the procrastination trap:** Easy tasks feel productive. But clearing easy tasks that don't move anything forward is false productivity. Identify the one most important leverage point, even if it's uncomfortable.

## Core Takeaways (Synthesis)

1. The PLG Iceberg gives you a sequential diagnostic. Work through layers top to bottom; don't optimize monetization when friction is broken.
2. Core problem communication and information for decision are often where big companies get lazy and where small companies can punch above their weight.
3. Friction is strategic — the right friction accelerates time to value; removing all friction often destroys it.
4. Value discovery is about smart defaults and contextual revelation, not onboarding checklists.
5. Your aha moment metric must predict retention, not just a surface action.
6. Repeatable value needs a low floor and high ceiling — power users are your best advocates and your stickiest customers.
7. Monetize in alignment with value delivery; never sell to users in-product — guide them to upgrade when they hit natural ceilings.
8. Flywheels are already in your business — identify, define, and accelerate the ones you have rather than inventing complex new ones.
9. PLG is not the right starting motion for every company. Sales-led growth early creates more learning, higher intent, and better PMF signals.
10. AB testing is a tool, not a strategy — apply it with high bars, clear hypotheses, and ruthless prioritization.
11. Strategy should be a compass, not a comfort zone. Execution beats strategy documents at every early-stage company.
12. Growth teams and core product teams must be deeply intertwined — the highway and on-ramp metaphor only works if both teams know the full road.
13. Trust is the PM's primary currency — earn it before shipping anything.
14. Find your biggest lever and pull it; comparative advantage thinking applied daily is the unlock for sustainable output without burnout.
